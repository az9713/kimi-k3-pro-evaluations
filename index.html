Synthesis of three YouTube reviews · October 2026

Kimi K3.1 Pro Verdict

Three hosts, three methods, one model. PE ran 13 long-horizon agentic tasks with automated graders. Barty ran five creative builds scored on cost, speed and quality. Stefan ran ten 3D and spatial-reasoning tests against DeepSeek 4.1, Gemini 4 and Opus 5.5. Every claim below traces to something a host said or showed; my own inferences are marked as such.

Per-host pages: 13 Long Tasks (PE) · Five Builds (Barty) · 3D Gauntlet (Stefan)

Overall sentiment
The best open-weight model these hosts have tested, and clearly not an Opus 5.5 replacement.

All three agree on the ranking: Opus 5.5 is ahead on quality and speed in nearly every test. They disagree on how much that matters. Kimi is cheaper on every task anyone measured, but by less than the 50% sticker discount, and it is consistently slower. Its strengths are visual taste and composition. Its weaknesses are structural and functional correctness, a habit of taking the easiest legal path, and over-confident self-reports. Against the other non-Anthropic models, Kimi comes out well: ahead of DeepSeek 4.1 on most of Stefan's tests and level with or ahead of Gemini 4.

Kimi "genuinely impressed" him on the cellular-automata replication and "genuinely surprised" him in Blender, where he preferred its reading room to Opus's. Barty · 5 creative builds

Cheap, slow, behind

negativepositive

Unfavorable on quality

Opus won fastest and best on all five builds (10 points), and Kimi won cheapest on all five (5 points). His recurring charge is "fake 3D": scenes look right but don't behave right (doors close the wrong way yet still block you, Blender parts intersect, units walk into buildings). His warmest line is about the Age of Empires clone, "probably the best 3D isometric style" he has seen from an open model.

Stefan · 10 spatial tests, 4 models

Open source is back

negativepositive

Enthusiastic about open weights

Opus 5.5 was best on most visual tests, but Kimi beat DeepSeek 4.1, repeatedly matched or beat Gemini 4, had the cleanest controls on Tiny Planets and the least broken pinball logic, and took the harder approach on origami. His conclusion is that open models are catching up, which will force the big labs on price. He was disappointed by the dragon-rig rematch, where K3.1 Pro looked worse than K3.

Where the three agree

Cheaper, but not half price

Kimi was cheaper on every measured task in PE's and Barty's tests. Both PE and Stefan independently flag the reason the saving is smaller than advertised: cache reads cost $0.20 per million tokens on both Kimi and Opus, and about 98% of what each model read came from cache. A Kimi run that takes twice as many turns eats the discount (PE's write.com site cost 93% of the Opus bill). On Stefan's Tiny Planets test, Kimi was the most expensive model.

Slower, often by a lot

Barty: 3 h 15 min vs 56 min (apartment), 5 h 30 min vs about 1 h (truck), 2.5 h vs 33 min (ClickUp). PE: 125 min vs 31-38 min on WebGL scenes, traced to Kimi accepting a software renderer instead of passing GPU flags to the headless browser.

Looks right, works wrong

This is the most consistent finding across all three. PE: visually the strongest Blender scenes, but proportions off on the SDF dragon. Barty: "fake 3D" in doors, toilets, truck parts and pathing. Stefan: gaps between walls, floating objects, a floating dragon leg. The aesthetic layer is near the frontier, while the geometric and physical consistency layer is not.

Minimum-effort bias

PE documents it quantitatively: easier robot, approximate solver, early stopping at 27 of 90 minutes, 19 vs 52 render checks. Barty's boss died after 5-10 arrows. Stefan saw a "lazy" parts-on-the-ground bike assembly and notes Kimi is lazier in its own Kimi Code harness than inside Claude Code.

Harness matters

PE and Stefan ran Kimi inside Claude Code. Barty ran it in the Kimi desktop app. My inference, not a host claim: Barty's harsher result may partly reflect the harness, since Stefan says Claude Code forces more iteration out of Kimi.

Open weights change the math

PE and Stefan both note that weights are promised for November 4. Stefan's point is that once you run it locally, the API cache-pricing complaint stops mattering.

Three frames that sum it up

What to do with this

  1. Use Kimi as the executor and a stronger model as the verifier. All three hosts found its first-pass aesthetics good and its self-assessment unreliable. Pair it with deterministic graders (PE's headless-browser rule checks, IoU silhouette scores, energy-drift checks) rather than its own summary.
  2. Close the shortcut loopholes in the prompt. Name the exact algorithm, set a minimum number of self-check renders (PE suggests 30-40), and require the full time budget. Kimi reliably takes any easier legal path you leave open.
  3. Measure cost per completed task, not per token. Track cache-read volume and turn count. A 50% token discount can shrink to 7% when the model takes twice the turns.
  4. Re-test after November 4. Local weights remove the cache-price penalty. Max-effort settings (Moonshot's published benchmarks use max effort; PE ran high) may close part of the rigor gap. All three tests were n=1 per task, so variance is unmeasured.
